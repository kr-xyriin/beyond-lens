Yes — this can work. The underlying problem is real, and the idea is stronger than it might initially sound.

But the model in its current form has a few structural problems that could kill it if you simply build “pay ₹500 → talk to a senior for 15 minutes.” The opportunity is less about selling calls and more about creating a trusted student-to-student college decision platform.

Here’s how I’d evaluate it.

The problem you're solving is legitimate
A Class 12 student considering a private college is making a decision that can easily involve ₹10–30+ lakh over 3–4 years, yet their information usually comes from college websites, admissions counselors, YouTube videos, Reddit/Quora, education portals, friends-of-friends, and random Instagram/LinkedIn DMs.

All of those have problems. College marketing is obviously biased. YouTube creators may have incentives. Reddit can be anonymous/outdated. LinkedIn requires cold-DMing strangers. And education portals often optimize around leads rather than brutally accurate student experience.

Your pitch is essentially:

“Before spending lakhs on a college, spend a few hundred rupees talking privately to someone who actually studies there.”

That's intuitive.

And importantly, you're not selling information that technically can't be found elsewhere. You're selling access + verification + convenience + personalization + trust.

That distinction matters.

Yes, they can sue or send a legal notice. That does not mean they would automatically win.

In India, defamation law can apply to companies and associations, not just individuals. Section 356 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita specifically says an imputation concerning a company or association can amount to defamation. But the same provision also contains important exceptions, including truth when publication is for the public good and certain good-faith opinions. 

So your goal should not be, “How do we make ourselves impossible to sue?” You can't. Your goal is:

How do we build this so that what happens on the platform is genuine first-hand student experience, rather than the platform itself making defamatory accusations?

The difference is huge
A senior saying:

“In my experience, hostel food was pretty bad and I stopped using the mess after first year.”

is very different from:

“XYZ Hostel scams every student and steals their money.”

Likewise:

“I personally found placement opportunities weaker than I expected.”

is safer than:

“The university lies about all its placements.”

The second type makes a concrete allegation of dishonest conduct. You'd want evidence before allowing something like that to be publicly published.

And this isn't just theoretical: Indian courts recognize both reputation and free-expression interests, including the significance of truth and fair comment in defamation disputes. 

I would design your startup around five protections
1. Make it clear that seniors speak for themselves, not for your company.

Every profile/call should prominently say something like:

“Views expressed are the individual student's personal experiences and opinions. They do not represent the university, hostel, property owner, or this platform.”

That disclaimer doesn't magically remove liability, but it helps establish what your service actually is.

2. Create strict rules for seniors.

They should be allowed to discuss their experience freely, including negative experiences, but prohibited from knowingly making false factual accusations.

For example:

“Professor quality wasn't great for me.” → generally fine as opinion.

“Most students in my batch didn't receive internships through campus.” → potentially okay if genuinely based on experience.

“The university fabricated its placement statistics.” → serious factual allegation; don't encourage this unless there is strong evidence.

“The hostel owner is a criminal.” → major red flag.

“The area has a terrible drug problem.” → potentially defamatory/dangerous if asserted as fact without evidence.

This becomes part of the senior's Terms of Service.

3. Don't turn sensational statements into your own marketing.

This is where I think you could accidentally increase your risk substantially.

Suppose a senior tells someone privately:

“My experience with placements wasn't good.”

That's one thing.

Now imagine your Instagram account publishes:

🚨 “UPES PLACEMENTS ARE TERRIBLE — CURRENT STUDENT EXPOSES THE TRUTH”

Now your company is publishing and amplifying the claim.

Very different risk profile.

Your marketing should be:

“Talk to verified UPES students about their experiences with placements.”

Not:

“Find out what UPES doesn't want you to know.”

I would stay miles away from “exposed”, “scam”, “fraud”, “fake placements”, “college lies”, etc. unless you're prepared to operate like an investigative publication with evidence and legal review.

Private calls are actually helpful for your model
Your original concept is safer than building a giant public review wall.

If a verified senior privately tells a prospective student:

“I pay ₹1.6 lakh for my hostel and I personally don't think it's worth it.”

that's materially different operationally from you publishing:

It does not mean private speech can never create legal problems, but the more your company edits, ranks, summarizes, headlines and publicly broadcasts accusations, the more you should think of yourself as a publisher rather than merely a connection platform.

India's IT Act contains a Section 79 safe-harbour framework for certain intermediaries hosting/transmitting third-party information, subject to conditions and due diligence.

But do not build your company assuming “Section 79 means we're protected.” Whether your exact marketplace qualifies and how the protection applies will depend on how you operate it. A startup lawyer should review this before launch.
